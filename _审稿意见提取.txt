5. Please describe the main contribution of the paper.
The paper proposes a vision-language framework, CLIP-CAT, for cataract grading that
integrates global semantic features from a pretrained CLIP encoder with locally
extracted fine-grained features via an adaptive patching mechanism, and introduces a
rank-aware loss to model the ordinal nature of disease severity. By combining these
components, the method aims to improve grading performance on small and
imbalanced clinical datasets.
6. Please list the major strengths of the paper. For example, you could highlight a
novel formulation, an original way to use data, a demonstration of clinical
feasibility, a novel application, a particularly strong evaluation, or anything else
that is a strong aspect of this work. Please provide details, for instance, if a
method is novel, explain what aspect is novel and why this is interesting.
1）Clinically motivated ordinal modeling: The paper explicitly incorporates the ordinal
nature of cataract grading via a rank-aware loss, which is well aligned with clinical
practice where the severity levels follow a natural progression and large grading errors
are more critical than minor ones.
2）Reasonable integration of global and local feature learning: The proposed dual-
branch design combines global representations from a pretrained CLIP encoder with
locally extracted fine-grained features, which is intuitively suitable for capturing both
overall lens structure and subtle opacity patterns under limited data conditions.

1. Background and Problem Setting
• Weak clinical motivation: The practical necessity of cataract grading based on slit-
lamp images is not sufficiently justified. The paper lacks discussion on real deployment
scenarios and does not clarify its advantages over other commonly used modalities
(e.g., OCTA or fundus imaging).
• Misleading “few-shot” claim: The paper does not follow a standard few-shot learning
protocol (e.g., N-way K-shot setting), and is essentially trained on a small-scale dataset,
making the claim potentially misleading.
• Incomplete related work: The review of existing cataract grading and ordinal modeling
methods is insufficient, leading to unclear positioning of the proposed approach within
the literature.
2. Methodological Limitations
• Limited methodological novelty: The overall framework lacks a coherent design
principle, and the components (global branch, local branch, text alignment, and ordinal
loss) appear loosely connected, resembling an engineering combination rather than a
unified modeling framework.
• Questionable contribution of vision-language modeling: The prompt design is tightly
coupled with class labels and essentially functions as a textual reformulation of labels.
There is insufficient evidence that the model achieves genuine semantic understanding.
• Lack of medical justification for local modeling: The entropy-based patch selection
strategy lacks clinical grounding. Cataracts primarily manifest as opacity changes within
the lens region, rather than increased texture complexity. High-entropy regions may
arise from illumination variations, reflections, or noise, and do not necessarily
correspond to pathological areas.
• Unclear source of performance gains: The observed improvements may largely stem
from the use of a stronger pretrained backbone and ordinal loss, rather than the
claimed semantic reasoning mechanism.
3. Limited data scale with risk of overfitting: The dataset contains only approximately
2,000 images from a single center. Under such conditions, the model may overfit to
fixed prompts and label structures, rather than learning robust representations.
4. Experimental Design and Evaluation
• Insufficient comparison: The set of compared methods is limited and does not include
recent or task-specific approaches, making it difficult to convincingly demonstrate
superiority.
• Incomplete analysis: The paper lacks per-grade performance evaluation and
confusion matrix analysis, making it unclear how the model performs across different
severity levels.
• Limited validation scope: Experiments are conducted on a single dataset only, with no
broader validation to support the robustness of the conclusions.
5. Presentation and Structural Issues
• Inconsistency between figures and text: Fig. 1 includes components (e.g., multiple
