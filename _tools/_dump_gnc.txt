L015: Abstract
L016: 
L017: Cataract is the leading cause of reversible blindness worldwide, yet automated grading remains challenging due to limited labeled data, severe class imbalance, and the inherently ordinal progression of the disease. Existing convolutional neural network-based approaches commonly treat cataract grading as nominal classification, overlooking the ordinal disease structure and under-utilizing medical prior knowledge. We propose CLIP-CAT, a vision-language model that leverages medical text prompts to alleviate data scarcity and improve grading performance. Our approach combines language-guided global semantic representations and localized fine-grained feature learning in a unified framework. To mitigate the limitations of global encoders in detecting subtle lesions, we introduce Adaptive Patching for enhanced local representation. In addition, we incorporate a Bidirectional Rank-Aware Loss that encourages unimodal probability distributions, effectively modeling the inherent ordinality of the task. Experiments on 1,673 slit-lamp images demonstrate that CLIP-CAT achieves 86.76% accuracy and 0.42 mean squared error, outperforming generic backbones and task-specific grading methods. Per-grade analysis and five-fold cross-validation confirm that errors concentrate on adjacent grades and that performance is stable across data splits.
L039: 2.2 Vision-Language Models in Medicine
L040: 
L041: Vision-language models such as CLIP (8) align images and text in a shared embedding space. Subsequent work scales contrastive pretraining and data pipelines (12-22), extends objectives toward multimodal generation and matching (23-25), and develops prompt-based and adapter-based adaptation for downstream transfer (26-39). In medicine, these models have mainly been applied in radiology and pathology, while ophthalmic adoption remains limited. In cataract assessment, deep grading systems built on clinical scales have been developed for slit-lamp and retro-illumination photographs (40), nuclear cataract diagnosis from slit-lamp video (41), and cortical cataract grading with clinical sub-scores (42), but these pipelines remain CNN- or Transformer-based classifiers without language-aligned semantics. Standard contrastive alignment, moreover, does not preserve the ordinal topology of cataract severity. Unlike prior pipelines that attach ordinal losses to convolutional neural network features (4,6,7), this study couples vision-language alignment with a Bidirectional Rank-Aware Loss and grade-specific medical prompts.
L053: Bidirectional Rank-Aware Loss: To address the ordinal nature of cataracts, we integrate a distance-weighted rank loss that penalizes predictions based on their expected ordinal distance from the true grade, encouraging a unimodal probability distribution.
L054: 
L055: Given a slit-lamp image x, the model predicts the cataract severity grade G ∈ {G0, G1, G2, G3, G4, G5}, where the severity is computed as the average of the nuclear color grade (NC, ranging from NC1 to NC6) and the cortical opacity grade (C, ranging from C0 to C5), both following the LOCS III standard (2), rounded up to the nearest integer. Specifically, with NC and C encoded as integers from 0 to 5 (NC1 to NC6 encoded as 0 to 5; C0 to C5 encoded as 0 to 5), the severity grade is G = ⌈(NC + C)/2⌉. Higher grades indicate more severe cataracts.
L056: 
L057: Our objective is to predict a probability distribution that not only maximizes accuracy but also respects the ordinal topology of the disease. Specifically, the model must minimize the expectation of the ordinal distance loss, ensuring that severe cross-grade errors (for example, confusing Grade 0 with Grade 4) are penalized more heavily than adjacent errors (6).
L113: The complete training objective combines a focal term and the Bidirectional Rank-Aware Loss (hereafter the rank loss). Let g* ∈ {0,1,2,3,4,5} denote the ground-truth grade encoded as an integer, and p(g|x) the predicted probability distribution from §3.3.3. The focal component is L_focal = -(1 - p(g*|x))^γ log p(g*|x), where γ=2 down-weights well-classified samples so that hard tail-grade cases dominate the gradient (43). The rank loss penalizes the expected ordinal distance: L_rank = Σ_{g<g*} p(g|x)·(g*-g) + Σ_{g>g*} p(g|x)·(g-g*). The first sum charges probability mass placed on grades below the true grade (underestimation), and the second charges mass placed above it (overestimation), both weighted linearly by grade distance. By construction, L_rank equals the expected absolute distance between the predicted distribution and the ground-truth grade, placing it in the established family of expected-distance ordinal objectives (6,7); its bidirectional character reflects the symmetry of the absolute distance, which charges under- and overestimation alike, rather than a new loss form. The total loss is L_total = L_focal + λ·L_rank, where λ=1.0 balances the two terms. In the ablation study (Table 2), the Imbalance Strategy switch jointly enables class-weighted sampling and the focal term; rows without it train with uniform sampling and the standard cross-entropy term in place of L_focal. This combined objective mitigates class imbalance via the focal term while enforcing ordinal structure via the rank term, ensuring that even when misclassifications occur, predictions remain clustered within the neighborhood of the true grade. As illustrated in Figure 2, mass on grades below g* incurs an underestimation penalty, and mass above incurs an overestimation penalty, both weighted by ordinal distance.

L115: 3.5 Dataset Description and Ethics Statement
L116: 
L117: To validate our framework, we curated a private clinical dataset of 1,673 slit-lamp images from 1,673 distinct patients (one image per patient). The cohort included patients across all age stages and of both sexes. All images were collected via routine clinical slit-lamp photography; the specific slit-lamp models and acquisition parameters were not recorded. Ground truth was established by three senior ophthalmologists via consensus voting: each image was graded by all three graders, and the final label was determined by consensus. As the reference standard reflects consensus rather than independent parallel ratings, inter-rater κ is not reported. Each image carries both a cortical grade (C0 to C5) and a nuclear color grade (NC1 to NC6) on the LOCS III scale. This study targets joint severity grading, where each case is labeled with both a nuclear color grade (NC1 to NC6) and a cortical opacity grade (C0 to C5). The final severity level G ∈ {G0, G1, G2, G3, G4, G5} is computed as the average of the nuclear color grade and the cortical opacity grade, rounded up to the nearest integer: with NC and C encoded as integers from 0 to 5 (NC1 to NC6 as 0 to 5; C0 to C5 as 0 to 5), G = ⌈(NC + C)/2⌉. The dataset exhibits a severe long-tailed distribution (Figure 4): the two head grades (NC2 and NC3) comprise 78.1% of samples, the mildest grade (NC1) accounts for 13.7%, and the two most severe grades (NC5 and NC6) together cover only 2.1%. Without intervention, this imbalance risks biasing the model toward the majority class to minimize global loss, thereby failing to identify severe, sight-threatening cases.
L118: 
L119: This study used a private, non-public dataset of cataract case photographs provided by a participating hospital. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shenyang Fourth People's Hospital (approval no. 2026-ZFKY-008) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Written informed consent for the use of the clinical images in research was obtained from the participating patients or their legally authorized representatives.
L127: Prototype smoothing maintains momentum-updated class centers to stabilize training under small batch sizes and class imbalance. For each grade g, we maintain a running-average prototype vector c_g ∈ R^768. After each batch, c_g is updated via exponential moving average: c_g ← m·c_g + (1-m)·(1/|B_g|)Σ_{x∈B_g} f_fused(x), where B_g is the set of samples with ground-truth grade g in the current batch, m=0.9 is the momentum coefficient, and f_fused(x) is the fused visual feature from §3.2.3. These prototypes are used to compute an auxiliary contrastive term (not included in the final loss) that encourages intra-class compactness during training. Long-tailed training instability is also addressed by uniformity-preserving transfer for visual prompt tuning (45).
L128: 
L129: Weighted sampling addresses the long-tailed distribution by oversampling minority grades. Class weights are set inversely proportional to sample frequency: w_g = N / (C·n_g), where N=1673 is the total dataset size, C=6 is the number of grades, and n_g is the number of samples in grade g. The per-grade counts are {n_0=229, n_1=758, n_2=549, n_3=102, n_4=20, n_5=15}, summing to 1673, which yields weights {w_0=1.22, w_1=0.37, w_2=0.51, w_3=2.73, w_4=13.94, w_5=18.59}. During training, each sample x with ground-truth grade g is drawn with probability proportional to w_g. This ensures that rare severe grades (G4, G5) appear more frequently in training batches, counteracting the natural imbalance.
L159: 4.1 Comparison with Baseline and Task-Specific Methods
L161: We benchmarked our CLIP-CAT against generic convolutional neural network backbones (ResNet50, DenseNet121), a Transformer backbone (ViT-B/16), CLIP transfer baselines (zero-shot and linear probing), an ordinal regression baseline (ResNet50 plus CORAL), and two task-specific cataract grading methods (CDRFF-Net and a LOCS III-based hybrid), corresponding to the backbone, transfer, ordinal, and task-specific methods described above (4,5,7-11). The quantitative results are summarized in Table 1.
L163: CLIP-CAT outperforms the strongest baseline (CDRFF-Net, 83.10%) by 3.66 percentage points in accuracy (86.76% versus 83.10%) and exceeds the best generic backbone (ViT-B/16, 82.55%) by 4.21 percentage points. A nonparametric bootstrap 95% confidence interval for CLIP-CAT accuracy (10,000 resamples of the 335 test images) is [83.3%, 90.4%]. While convolutional neural network architectures like ResNet50 provide a reasonable baseline (78.45%), their relatively high expected-grade mean squared error (1.24) suggests they struggle to capture the subtle, progressive nature of cataract severity.
L165: A critical contribution of our method is the reduction in expected-grade mean squared error (MSE_exp) to 0.42. In the context of ordinal regression, MSE_exp reflects the distance between the ground truth and the probabilistic expectation of the predicted distribution. An MSE_exp of 0.42 implies an average prediction deviation of approximately 0.65 grades. This indicates a bounded error property: even when CLIP-CAT misclassifies a sample, the predicted probability distribution typically centers on the immediate neighboring grade, rather than making catastrophic errors. The corresponding discrete-grade MSE (MSE_hard, computed from argmax predictions) is 0.17, reflecting that 90.9% of hard prediction errors land on adjacent grades (see Section 4.3). This result does not establish clinical safety; it only describes the error pattern observed in this dataset and should not be interpreted as evidence of readiness for clinical use. Furthermore, the high Kappa coefficient (0.88) is consistent with strong agreement between model outputs and the study's expert-derived labels under the reported evaluation design (6).
L167: Applying CLIP (8) in a zero-shot manner yields only 64.20% accuracy, and a linear probe on frozen CLIP features reaches 79.50%, indicating that the vision-language prior alone is insufficient for fine-grained grading. Equipping ResNet50 with the CORAL ordinal objective (7) raises accuracy to 80.30% and reduces mean squared error from 1.24 to 0.90, consistent with the value of ordinal modeling in this experiment, yet it remains well below CLIP-CAT. Among task-specific methods, CDRFF-Net (4) and the LOCS III-based hybrid model (5) achieve 83.10% and 82.65%, respectively. Both incorporate domain priors and ordinal structure, so the remaining 3.66 percentage point accuracy advantage and lower mean squared error (0.42 versus 0.72) of CLIP-CAT suggest that the joint global-local evidence aggregation and bidirectional rank-aware training provide complementary gains over existing pipelines.
L169: 4.2 Ablation Study
L171: To verify the contribution of each component in our framework, we conducted a step-by-step ablation study. The results, detailed in Table 2, demonstrate the additive value of each module.
L173: The CLIP Base configuration (Row 1)—the global CLIP ViT-L/14 branch with grade-specific medical prompts, fine-tuned as described in Section 3.6 with the local branch, rank loss, and imbalance handling all disabled—yields an accuracy of 80.15%, distinct from the zero-shot (64.20%) and linear-probe (79.50%) transfer settings in Table 1. While promising, the domain gap between natural images and slit-lamp photography limits its precision. Incorporating the Adaptive Patching Local Branch improves accuracy to 82.40%. This confirms that fine-grained local features are essential for capturing early-stage lesions that are often lost in global semantic tokens. The integration of the rank loss provides the most significant improvement in error control, reducing the mean squared error from 0.92 to 0.55. This validates that explicitly penalizing non-ordinal predictions forces the model to learn the topological order of severity, effectively eliminating large-span outliers. Finally, combining the weighted sampling and Focal Loss strategies addresses the long-tail issue, further boosting the accuracy to 86.76% and achieving the lowest mean squared error of 0.42.
L175: Replacing entropy-based top-K selection with random patch sampling lowers accuracy from 82.40% to 81.30% in the two-module setting and from 86.76% to 85.92% in the full model, while slightly increasing mean squared error. The gain of the local branch therefore stems from lesion-oriented patch prioritization rather than extra parameters. The entropy statistic is used here as an image-selection heuristic, not as a validated physiological marker. In this dataset, patches with high texture heterogeneity concentrate on the lens opacity region and are selected as the top-K informative local patches without manual annotation. L179: 4.3 Per-Grade Performance and Error Analysis
L181: Aggregate metrics do not reveal how errors distribute across the severity scale, which is important for evaluating deployment readiness. We therefore report per-grade precision, recall, and F1 score (Table 3) together with the 6 × 6 confusion matrix (Table 4) on the held-out test subset (335 slit-lamp images, reserved as a stratified 20% test split).
L183: The three head grades G0 to G2 (NC1 to NC3), which together comprise 91.9% of the test set, reach F1 scores of 91.5%, 92.0%, and 85.8%, whereas G3 (NC4) falls to 60.9%, echoing the sample scarcity. The weighted F1 (87.3%) stays close to the overall accuracy, while the macro F1 (74.5%) quantifies the residual long-tail gap. The two most severe grades retain only 4 and 3 test images, so their estimates (F1 50.0% and 66.7%) carry wide confidence intervals (exact binomial 95% CI for recall: G4 75.0% [19.4%–99.4%]; G5 66.7% [9.4%–99.2%]).
L185: Among the 44 misclassified test images, 40 (90.9%) land on immediately adjacent grades, 4 (9.1%) deviate by two grades, and none deviate by three or more. This error distribution substantiates the bounded error property: the corresponding discrete-grade MSE (MSE_hard) computed from the confusion matrix is 0.17, calculated as (40×1² + 4×2²)/335 = 56/335 ≈ 0.167. The expected-grade MSE (MSE_exp = 0.42, reported in Table 1) is higher because it measures the squared distance between the ground truth and the probabilistic expectation Σ_g g·p(g|x), which accounts for probability mass distributed across multiple grades rather than a single hard prediction. Both metrics confirm that CLIP-CAT's predictions remain concentrated near the true grade. Errors lean toward over-grading (27 versus 17), so the clinically riskier direction, under-grading of Grades 4 to 5, occurs in only 2 test images (0.6%), and every such deviation remains within one grade. Confusions concentrate between neighboring mid-grades (for example, G2↔G3, 13 cases: 10 + 3), which matches the continuous progression of nuclear opacity and explains why the rank-aware term remains effective. L187: 4.4 Generalizability and Limitations
L189: Single-center datasets with severe long tails pose a genuine risk of overfitting, and the absolute scale of our cohort (1,673 images) is modest by contemporary standards. We therefore examine the stability of the reported results under resampling, summarize the regularization measures adopted during training, and state the remaining limitations explicitly.
L191: To quantify split sensitivity, we repeated the evaluation with 5-fold cross-validation over the full dataset: CLIP-CAT attains 86.1% ± 0.85% accuracy, a mean squared error of 0.45 ± 0.05, and a Kappa of 0.87 ± 0.02 across folds. The mean accuracy closely tracks the single-split result of 86.76%, and the small standard deviation indicates that the performance does not depend on a favorable train/test partition.
L193: Several mechanisms constrain overfitting on this data scale. Only the upper 12 transformer blocks of the CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder, the attention fusion layer, and the projection layers are trainable, while the first 12 blocks and the text encoder remain fixed, reducing the number of parameters that must be learned from 1,171 training images. Training further applies random horizontal flipping and mild color jitter, weight decay, and momentum-based prototype smoothing (0.9) for stable class centers. The checkpoint with the best validation performance is selected for evaluation. The tight cross-validation variance and the consistent ranking of modules in Table 2 suggest that the gain stems from architectural priors rather than dataset-specific memorization.
L195: All results reported in this work are obtained on one institution's slit-lamp images. A multi-center validation protocol is under preparation: the trained model will be applied without fine-tuning to cohorts collected from other centers and grading workflows, and any performance drift will be analyzed with the same per-grade and confusion-matrix methodology. External validation results are not yet available, and we restrict our claims accordingly.
L197: This study has four concrete limitations. First, the cohort contains 1,673 single-center images, so device-specific and population-specific biases cannot be excluded. Second, the long-tailed distribution leaves the two most severe grades with only 4 and 3 test images, so their per-grade estimates carry wide confidence intervals despite the bounded-error behavior. Third, although labels follow LOCS III consensus grading by three senior ophthalmologists, comorbidities (for example, corneal opacity) may still confound appearance-based grading, and external multi-center validation remains outstanding. Fourth, the baseline comparison does not include medical vision-language models such as BiomedCLIP, MedCLIP, and PubMedCLIP. These models are pretrained on radiology, pathology, or biomedical figure-caption corpora rather than slit-lamp anterior-segment photography, so zero-shot transfer to LOCS III grading would conflate imaging-modality mismatch with representation quality. A fair comparison would require fine-tuning each backbone under the same long-tailed protocol, which is beyond the scope of the present limited-data study; our zero-shot CLIP and linear-probe baselines instead isolate the contribution of vision-language pretraining and supervised adaptation within the target modality. We leave the comparison with domain-specific medical vision-language backbones to future multi-center work.
